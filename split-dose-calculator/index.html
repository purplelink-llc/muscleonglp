Splitting a weekly dose is not the labeled schedule. Ozempic and Zepbound are labeled for one injection a week, and we know of no published trial of splitting a weekly dose into smaller injections, so it has not been shown to be safer, better tolerated or more effective. This tool only does the division. Tell your prescriber before you change how you inject, and ask your pharmacist whether your pen or vial can deliver the smaller dose.

How this is calculated

The dose. Each injection is the weekly total divided by the number of injections you pick, shown in milligrams to three decimals. Under the schedule the calculator adds the injections back up so you can see the weekly total is unchanged.

The days. Injections are spread as evenly as a seven-day week allows, counting from your first injection day. Two injections fall three and four days apart, three fall two, two and three days apart, and seven are daily.

What it leaves out. It works in milligrams only. It does not convert to syringe units or milliliters, because for a vial that depends on the concentration printed on the label and a mistake there is the dangerous kind. It knows nothing about your device, your dose history or your prescriber's instructions.

What the labels say

The Ozempic label says to administer once weekly on the same day each week, at any time of day, with or without meals, and gives a half-life of approximately 1 week.1 The Zepbound label says once weekly, at any time of day, tells patients to take a missed dose within 4 days (96 hours) or skip it, and gives a half-life of approximately 5 to 6 days.2 The Zepbound label also lists single-dose pens and single-dose vials that each hold one labeled dose, and a multi-dose vial and KwikPen that hold four.2 The missed-dose rules above belong to once-weekly dosing; they do not carry over to a split schedule.

What is not known

Searches for a split dose calculator turn up many tools and many clinic blogs. What they do not turn up is trial data. The approved labels and the trials behind them used one injection a week, and we know of no published study of splitting a weekly dose into smaller injections. That does not make it dangerous, and it does not make it useful. It means nobody has measured it, so any claim that a split schedule is gentler or works better is a guess. If your prescriber recommended a split schedule, this page is a way to check the arithmetic. If you are considering one yourself, the conversation to have first is with them.

Honest limits

The division is exact; everything around it is not. Rounding can leave the total a hair off, your device may not be able to deliver the smaller dose, and a schedule that is not on the label is one your prescriber and insurer may not expect. Treat the result as a way to talk to your prescriber, not a plan.

Frequently asked

How do I split a weekly Ozempic or GLP-1 dose?

Divide the weekly total your prescriber set by the number of injections, then space the injections as evenly as the week allows: two injections land on days 0 and 3 (for example Monday and Thursday). That is all this calculator does. Splitting is not the labeled schedule, so tell your prescriber before you change how you inject, and ask your pharmacist whether your pen or vial can deliver the smaller dose.

Is it safe to split a weekly GLP-1 dose into two injections?

It has not been shown to be. The Ozempic and Zepbound labels specify one injection a week, and we know of no published trial of splitting a weekly dose into smaller injections, so there is no evidence that it is safer, better tolerated or more effective. If your prescriber chose a split schedule for you, follow their instructions. If you are thinking about it on your own, ask them first.

Does splitting change the total weekly dose?

No. Two injections of half the weekly dose add up to the same total. The calculator shows that sum under the schedule, including when rounding to three decimals makes the total differ slightly.

Does this convert milligrams to units or milliliters?

No, deliberately. It works in milligrams only. Converting to syringe units or milliliters for a vial depends on the concentration printed on that vial, and a mistake there is the dangerous kind. Ask your pharmacist or prescriber for that step.

Does it work for tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound)?

The arithmetic is the same. The device is the catch: the Zepbound label lists single-dose pens and single-dose vials that each hold one labeled dose, so they cannot be divided, while its multi-dose vial and KwikPen hold four doses. Check your own product's label and ask your pharmacist.
