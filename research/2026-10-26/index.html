Changes in Muscle Strength Following Semaglutide Treatment in Adults: A Systematic Review With Meta-Analysis of Handgrip Strength.

Diabetes, obesity & metabolism · 2026-10-19 · Machado LG et al.

This PROSPERO-registered systematic review and meta-analysis pooled data from 10 studies (660 semaglutide-treated participants) to assess changes in skeletal muscle strength—primarily handgrip strength—during semaglutide treatment. The pooled within-group change in handgrip strength was nonsignificant (−0.16 kgf; 95% CI: −1.98 to 1.65), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 90.8%) and a wide prediction interval. The authors rated certainty of evidence as very low and concluded that available data are insufficient to determine whether semaglutide is associated with changes in handgrip strength, calling for more robust comparative studies with standardized assessments and longer follow-up.

Why it matters: This is the most direct available synthesis of muscle strength outcomes during semaglutide treatment, and its 'insufficient evidence' conclusion underscores how little is actually known about functional muscle changes on this drug.

What this means for you: This is one more reason to actively monitor grip strength and other functional measures during semaglutide treatment rather than assuming strength is being preserved.

Weight Regain After Incretin Treatment: The Lean Mass Deficit Hypothesis and a Role for Testosterone Combination Therapy in Men.

Diabetes, obesity & metabolism · 2026-10-19 · Zitzmann M et al.

This hypothesis paper proposes that in men with obesity and low or low-normal testosterone, combining incretin therapy with testosterone therapy may help preserve muscle mass, increase the proportion of weight lost as fat, and reduce fat-predominant weight regain after incretin discontinuation. The authors acknowledge that the only direct human data come from a small, non-randomized pilot study and are hypothesis-generating. They position resistance exercise and adequate protein intake as first-line measures for muscle preservation, and frame testosterone as a pharmacological adjunct requiring evaluation in randomized controlled trials before it can inform clinical practice.

Why it matters: The paper directly addresses the composition of incretin-induced weight loss and the clinical consequences of lean mass loss in men, proposing a testable framework for muscle preservation during and after GLP-1 therapy.

What this means for you: Worth discussing with a prescribing clinician, particularly for men with obesity who may have low testosterone and are concerned about muscle loss during incretin therapy.

New Horizons for Incretin-Based Therapeutics.

Annual review of physiology · 2026-10-23 · Liskiewicz D, Liskiewicz A, Müller TD

This narrative review in Annual Review of Physiology surveys the current and emerging landscape of incretin-based obesity pharmacotherapy, including receptor polyagonists, oral formulations, and small-molecule mimetics. It highlights that weight regain after treatment cessation remains a critical unresolved challenge. The review explicitly notes that growing evidence underscores the importance of sustained lifestyle interventions and muscle mass preservation to facilitate weight stabilization after treatment.

Why it matters: The review directly names muscle mass preservation as a key factor in preventing weight regain after GLP-1 therapy, situating it within the broader pharmacotherapy landscape.

What this means for you: This adds to the case for prioritizing resistance training and adequate protein intake throughout GLP-1 treatment, not just at the start.

Modern antidiabetic therapy in older adults with type 2 diabetes: evidence, best practice and a clinical pharmacist's framework.

Expert review of clinical pharmacology · 2026-10-22 · Martinc B et al.

This narrative review examines antidiabetic drug classes—including GLP-1 receptor agonists and tirzepatide—in older adults with type 2 diabetes, with attention to frailty, body composition, and functional vulnerability. The authors note that reductions in lean mass do not by themselves establish treatment-induced sarcopenia, and that weight-neutral therapy may be preferable when weight loss is undesirable. They call for structured medication reviews integrating nutrition, physical performance, and renal function, and flag that prospective validation in the oldest populations is still needed.

Why it matters: The paper directly addresses lean mass loss and sarcopenia risk in older adults on GLP-1 therapies, a population where functional decline from muscle loss carries heightened consequences.

What this means for you: Worth discussing with a prescribing clinician how physical performance and lean mass are being monitored, particularly for older adults on these medications.

Antidiabetic medications in the pipeline.

Hormones (Athens, Greece) · 2026-10-19 · Efstathiadou ZA et al.

This narrative review of the antidiabetic drug pipeline covers incretin-based multi-agonists, oral GLP-1 agonists, weekly insulins, and novel insulin sensitizers. Notably, it states that drugs specifically designed to restrain muscle mass loss caused by anti-obesity treatments are currently being tested, signaling an emerging pharmacological focus on lean mass preservation as a distinct therapeutic target.

Why it matters: The explicit mention of drugs in development to counter anti-obesity-treatment-induced muscle loss indicates that lean mass preservation is becoming a recognized clinical priority in the pipeline.
